How it works

01

Employees share what they observe.

If your organization invites you to participate, you answer structured questions about your experience at work. Participation is voluntary. Use a personal device and connection, away from work accounts, machines, and networks.

02

Yootle brings the answers together.

At least 30 responses are pooled before reporting results. Employers receive aggregate findings, never individual answers.

03

Employees get the findings, too.

All eligible employees receive the same findings within 24 hours of release to company management, whether or not they participated. The findings reflect the people who answered.

Solve Order

The argument behind the diagnosis

The Solve Order paper argues that companies improve their odds of success by giving priority to the people and conditions that produce their results. Employees’ experience is a starting point for understanding where those foundations are strong and where they need attention.

Think of it as reality-as-a-service: a way to ground the company’s understanding in what its employees observe.

Revenue, profit, EBITDA, customer satisfaction, and other performance measures tell a company how it is doing. Solve Order calls these results WHERE. Its hypothesis directs attention to five connected areas that help produce those results, in this priority order:

  1. WHOPut your people first.
  2. WHOMKnow your customers.
  3. WHYSet a clear direction.
  4. HOWMake good work repeatable.
  5. WHATDeliver products and services that fit.

Watching the scoreboard does not improve the score. Solve Order proposes the order in which to attend to what produces it. Yootle gathers candid observations from the people doing that work, so the company can examine how those five areas may be affecting its results.

Claims & experience

Does the company’s account match employees’ experience?

What the company says↔What employees observe

Yootle also measures the distance between a company’s stated claims and employees’ experience. These comparisons show where the company’s account of itself is supported by employees’ observations, and where the two diverge. Knowing there is a gap gives the company something concrete to investigate and address.

Together, the five-area assessment and these comparisons give the company a starting point for diagnosis: what appears to be working, where experiences differ, and what deserves closer investigation.
